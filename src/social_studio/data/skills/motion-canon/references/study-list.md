# Study list — 20 landmark pieces

For each: what it is, and the one technique to actually borrow for a code-driven motion piece. Full citations are in `../../research-note.md` section 5 — this file is the "what to take from it," not the research record.

1. **Vertigo (1958)** — Take: a single strong geometric motif (the spiral) driving the entire sequence's motion language, rather than a grab-bag of effects.
2. **North by Northwest (1959)** — Take: type treated as architecture — letters that resolve into the literal set/environment, not just text over a background.
3. **Se7en (1995)** — Take: deliberately rough, one-frame-cut texture used to build dread/tension — a reminder that grain/jitter can be a storytelling choice, not just a filter.
4. **Game of Thrones opening (2011)** — Take: a recurring "map" device that re-orients the viewer every time, built from a consistent mechanical/physical motion language (gears, not glossy UI).
5. **True Detective S1 (2014)** — Take: compositing two unrelated image sources (character + landscape) through transparency to say something neither says alone — a model for a double-exposure or blend-mode beat.
6. **Stranger Things main title (2016)** — Take: an intentionally imperfect practical technique (in-camera shake) kept rather than cleaned up, because the imperfection sells the era/mood.
7. **Captain America: Civil War titles (2016)** — Take: typography that casts story-relevant shadows — a motivated use of a lighting effect rather than decorative.
8. **Beyond Magic (2016, BUCK)** — Take: broadcast-grade graphics-package discipline (consistent HUD/lower-third language) applied across a full program, useful as a reference for this brief's HUD beat.
9. **Severance titles (2022/2025)** — Take: the opposite lesson from a fast-cut showreel — a single sustained idea given a long, slow hold can be more unsettling/premium than rapid cutting. Useful as a reminder that pace is a choice per-project, not a default.
10. **Premier League on-air package (DixonBaxi)** — Take: deriving a motion grammar from the subject itself (the sport's own movement) rather than applying a generic broadcast template — the method, not the specific graphics.
11. **More4 rebrand (ManvsMachine)** — Take: a bold, award-winning identity built from a small number of repeated motion motifs rather than many one-off effects.
12. **Spotify Wrapped** — Take: a data-viz/counter-roll-up motion system designed to scale across huge data variance while staying visually consistent — directly relevant to this brief's counter and donut beats.
13. **Universal Everything "Voxel Posse"** — Take: voxel/particle figures as a generative system (rules + randomness) rather than hand-keyframed — the direct precedent for a Three.js voxel-ripple beat.
14. **Call of Duty: Infinite Warfare UI (Ash Thorp)** — Take: HUD elements (brackets, readouts) designed as *diegetic* props that feel like they belong to a functioning system, not graphic overlays — informs the corner-bracket/SMPTE-timecode HUD beat.
15. **Deserve Cards visual language (Tendril)** — Take: a modular 3D system built explicitly to be re-cut across many formats/durations — a model for building reusable beat "modules" rather than one-off animations.
16. **Nike "Podium" (Instrument)** — Take: a brand/motion system built for scale across huge surface area — relevant to keeping a consistent look across many beats in one 15-30s piece.
17. **Linear launch films** — Take: quiet, fast, mostly-linear UI-native motion (80-120ms cuts) as a legitimate alternative register to broadcast bombast — useful specifically for the glass-UI-card beat in this brief, where motion should feel native to software, not cinematic.
18. **Vercel's motion-grammar brand system** — Take: a restrained color palette (black/white + one accent) where the *motion system itself* carries most of the visual interest — relevant when the palette is constrained (as this brief's ink/orange/cream/blue palette is).
19. **Theatre.js r3f demos** — Take: a visual-timeline workflow for 3D camera/material animation — a reference for how to think about camera moves even if ultimately hand-coded in GSAP.
20. **Motion Canvas example scenes** — Take: "code as timeline" (generator functions driving frame-exact vector animation synced to narration) — the closest open precedent to a deterministic, frame-rendered, code-driven data-viz beat.

## How to use this list

Before building a beat, pick the 1-2 entries above whose *technique* (not whose industry or budget) matches what the beat needs, and name explicitly what's being borrowed (e.g. "borrowing #12's counter-roll-up consistency, not its specific visual style"). This keeps study a tool for sharpening the idea rather than an excuse to copy a look wholesale.
